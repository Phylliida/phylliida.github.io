Burning the tight extremal Pk-free graphs

Pick a target burning number n, choose which extremal family to look at, and watch the optimal single-source burning round by round. Both families come from arXiv:2402.18984v5; the comparison is the interesting bit.

About the two families

Antony & Chandran et al.'s upper bound says b(G) ≤ ⌈(k+1)/2⌉ for any connected Pk-free graph G. Two extremal-example families try to match it:

  • Paper's spider 𝒫r: one branching vertex, r legs of length r−1. Has r(r−1)+1 = r²−r+1 vertices, is P2r-free, and has burning number exactly r. Indexed against k: setting r = ⌊k/2⌋ gives a Pk-free graph with b = ⌊k/2⌋ — one short of the upper bound ⌈(k+1)/2⌉. Hence the paper says "tight up to additive 1."
  • Our G̃t(m): Km,m between inner vertices x1,…,xm and y1,…,ym, with a length-t pendant path attached to each inner vertex. Has 2m(t+1) vertices, is P2t+4-free, and (when m ≥ t+3) has burning number exactly t+3 — matching the upper bound exactly. Taking t=1, m=4 recovers the paper's special "Fig. 3a" graph G̃.

Why only one source? In the burning protocol you do light a new source every round (one per round, on an unburned vertex if one is available). But both of these graphs are single-source-optimal: the highlighted source has eccentricity exactly b−1, so its expanding fire reaches every vertex by round b without needing help. The protocol still requires sources b2, …, bb, but they can be chosen arbitrarily on the unburned set — they don't make the burning shorter, so we omit them visually. See proof below.
Definition: Pk-free, and why our families are Pk-free

Pk denotes the path on k vertices: distinct vertices v1, …, vk with edges vivi+1 for i = 1, …, k−1 and no others. A graph G is Pk-free if no k-vertex subset S ⊆ V(G) has its induced subgraph G[S] isomorphic to Pk. Equivalently, every sequence of k distinct vertices either fails to have consecutive vertices adjacent, or has a "chord" — an edge between two non-consecutive vertices in the sequence.

Lemma (spider). The spider 𝒫r with r legs of length r−1 is P2r-free, and contains an induced P2r−1.

Proof. An induced P2r−1: walk from a leaf tip on one leg through the centre to a leaf tip on another leg. That gives (r−1) + 1 + (r−1) = 2r−1 vertices in a row; no chords exist because vertices on different legs are non-adjacent except through the centre, and consecutive chain vertices are the only same-leg adjacencies.

No longer induced path exists. Any induced path that meets more than one leg has to pass through the centre (the only point of contact between legs), and the centre can only be entered and exited once on a simple path. So an induced path uses at most 2 legs plus the centre, contributing at most (r−1) + 1 + (r−1) = 2r−1 vertices. Hence 𝒫r is P2r-free. ∎

Lemma (G̃t(m)). For m ≥ 2, G̃t(m) is P2t+4-free, and contains an induced P2t+3.

Proof. An induced P2t+3: for distinct a, c ∈ [m] and any b ∈ [m], the sequence xa(t) – xa(t−1) – … – xa – yb – xc – xc(1) – … – xc(t) has t + 1 + 1 + 1 + t = 2t+3 vertices. Each consecutive pair is an edge. No chords: pendant vertices are only adjacent to their chain neighbours; xa and xc are both on the x-side of the bipartition so non-adjacent; xayb and ybxc are already in the path.

No longer induced path exists. Look at any induced path P in G̃t(m) and its inner trace — the sub-sequence of P's vertices that lie in Km,m (i.e. at chain position 0). This inner trace is an induced path inside Km,m. But the longest induced path in a complete bipartite graph has at most 3 vertices: any sequence u1 – u2 – u3 – u4 with u1, u3 on one side and u2, u4 on the other forces the chord u1u4. So the inner trace has ≤ 3 vertices.

The rest of P consists of pendant chain segments attached at the inner trace's two endpoints (the only way to leave the Km,m bipartite core). Each such tail has ≤ t pendant vertices (the chain has only t vertices beyond its inner attachment). So |P| ≤ t + 3 + t = 2t + 3, hence G̃t(m) is P2t+4-free. ∎

This is the punchline: at any fixed k, take the family that is Pk-free and look at the burning number it forces. The spider sets r = ⌊k/2⌋ and gets b = ⌊k/2⌋; G̃t(m) sets t = (k−4)/2 (taking k even) and gets b = t+3 = (k+2)/2 = ⌈(k+1)/2⌉ — exactly the upper bound.

Proof that both families are single-source optimal

A graph G is single-source optimal iff b(G) = rad(G) + 1, where rad(G) is the radius (the minimum, over all vertices v, of v's eccentricity). The inequality b(G) ≤ rad(G) + 1 holds for every connected graph — pick a centre v, light it first, and the fire fills every distance-j shell by round j+1, finishing by round ecc(v) + 1 = rad(G) + 1. So single-source optimality is purely a question of whether the matching lower bound b(G) ≥ rad(G) + 1 holds.

Lemma (spider). For the spider 𝒫r with r ≥ 2 legs of length r−1, b(𝒫r) = r.

Proof. The radius is achieved at the centre c (every vertex is within distance r−1 of c; every non-centre vertex has a non-centre vertex at distance more than r−1), so rad(𝒫r) = r − 1 and the upper bound gives b ≤ r.

For the lower bound, suppose a burning sequence b1, …, br−1 of length r−1 worked. The spider has r leaf tips (one per leg), pairwise at distance 2(r−1). Each tip ℓ must be covered by some bi at distance ≤ (r−1)−i. By pigeonhole on r tips into r−1 sources, two leaf tips ℓ1, ℓ2 share a source bi: d(bi, ℓ1) ≤ r−1−i and d(bi, ℓ2) ≤ r−1−i. The triangle inequality then gives 2(r−1) = d(ℓ1, ℓ2) ≤ 2(r−1−i), forcing i ≤ 0, which is impossible (sources are indexed from 1). So b ≥ r. ∎

Lemma (G̃t(m)). If m ≥ t+3 then b(G̃t(m)) = t + 3.

Proof. An inner vertex xa has distances 0 to itself, s to its own pendant xa(s), 1 to any yj, 1+s to any yj(s), 2 to a different inner xb, and 2+s to its pendant xb(s). Maximum: t+2 (at xb(t), b ≠ a). So ecc(xa) = t + 2; you can check every other vertex has eccentricity at least this big, hence rad = t + 2 and b ≤ t + 3.

For the lower bound, suppose b1, …, bt+2 of length t+2 worked. Look at the m "y-side tips" TY = {yc(t) : c ∈ [m]}. For each tip yc(t), the closed ball of radius d around it is: just the chain {yc(0), …, yc(t)} for d ≤ t, the chain together with every inner xk for d = t+1, and the chain together with the inner x's, their pendants and every yj for d = t+2.

For i ≥ 2, the depth budget t+2−i is at most t, so bi can only sit on a single y-chain, hence covers at most one tip in TY. For i=1 the depth budget is t+1, so b1 reaches some tip in TY iff it lies on that tip's chain or is one of the inner xk's. But each xk reaches only one y-tip too: d(xk, yc(t)) = t+1 forces xk-on-chain only when k=c, and going through cross-edges gives distance t+1 only for c=k. (Symmetrically for the x-side tips.)

So whatever b1 is, between the two tip-sides at least m−1 tips remain uncovered after the first source. The remaining t+1 sources cover at most t+1 tips on that side (one each, as argued above). Hence we need m−1 ≤ t+1, i.e. m ≤ t+2, contradicting m ≥ t+3. So no length-(t+2) burning sequence works, and b ≥ t + 3. ∎

Both proofs identify a set of "far apart" target vertices (leaf tips in the spider, pendant chain tips in G̃t(m)) and use a counting argument to show that no shorter burning sequence can cover them all. Since the radius witness already achieves the matching upper bound, the single source at the centre (resp. at any inner xa) is genuinely optimal — and the extra sources b2, … that the protocol forces you to add are pure formality.
